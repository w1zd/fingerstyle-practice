# Put percussion, thumb and practice rules earlier

The existing 336-day plan has a sound skeleton: a 50-minute session with a mid-session break, a melody-first solo block, a weekly learn-apply-repair-record cycle, and monthly milestones that must be passed on two separate days. Three changes would make it much better. First, **add explicit practice rules**: a clean-rep tempo rule, a "finish on correct reps" rule, micro-rests, and blocked-then-interleaved scheduling. The motor-learning evidence says *how* you practise predicts retention better than *how long*. Second, **re-sequence the technique strand toward the target style**. Thumb-alone bass should start in Month 2 rather than Month 4. Pinch, monotonic bass, palm muting and rest stroke need to be added. The backbeat slap should arrive around Month 6, whereas the current plan tells the learner to "skip percussion" in Month 10. Natural harmonics should move from optional in Month 9 to core in Month 6, and barres should progress to full F by Months 8–9. Third, **add a named repertoire ladder, a scale-degree ear strand, a number-system arranging method and a cue-based memorisation routine** so that Months 8–12 have concrete tools. Evidence quality is uneven, and this report labels it throughout: practice-strategy findings come from small music studies (piano, winds, brass) and non-music motor studies, and none are guitar-specific. Technique sequencing and repertoire rest on expert and teacher opinion. All specific numbers (3 clean reps, 4–6 BPM steps, month placements) are the researchers' own proposals and should be labelled "rule of thumb" in the app.

**How to read the labels.** **[Evidence]** means peer-reviewed studies, with strength noted. **[Expert]** means teachers, performing-arts medicine bodies, luthiers or publishers. **[Proposal]** means the researchers' own synthesis. The current plan was read from `app/curriculum.json`: the routine, weekly rotation, eight exercises, twelve `monthlyPlans` and the 0–2 scoring on timing, clarity, continuity, balance and relaxation.

## Strategy beats minutes: practice rules the plan is missing

The best-supported finding is that practice *behaviour* drives retention. **[Evidence, moderate]** In Duke, Simmons & Cash's study of 17 advanced pianists, next-day retention was unrelated to practice time or number of trials. The best performers did four things: they fixed errors immediately, identified their source, varied the tempo of problem spots systematically, and repeated targets until the errors disappeared ([JRME 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)). **[Evidence, small-N observational]** A 2025 analysis of six professional trumpeters found a shared strategy. They formed a clear intention before each attempt and spent about **43% of their time thinking rather than playing**. After a failure they changed something rather than repeating it. They "doable-ized" hard passages (slower, fewer notes, softer), kept them expressive, and added complexity back only while the success rate stayed high ([Bulletproof Musician on Killion & Duke](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-central-strategy-expert-musicians-use-to-practice-difficult-passages/)). **[Evidence, strong meta-analysis]** At the macro level, deliberate practice explains about **21% of variance in music performance** ([Macnamara et al. 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810)). Ericsson's rebuttal argues that estimate depends on how loosely "deliberate practice" is defined, and that the concept requires feedback ([Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)). The practical reading for a self-taught adult is that quality matters, individual differences are real, and something has to stand in for the teacher's feedback.

The blocked-versus-interleaved question has a nuanced answer for beginners. **[Evidence, moderate]** Carter & Grahn had advanced clarinetists alternate 3-minute segments. Next-day ratings favoured interleaving on average, though raters disagreed, and most players found it helped focus and error-spotting ([Frontiers 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)). Earlier music studies were inconsistent, and learners reliably *prefer* blocked practice even when interleaving teaches more ([Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-misinterpreted-effort-hypothesis-and-why-we-often-practice-less-effectively-than-we-could/)). **[Evidence, non-music]** In novice basketball shooters, blocked repetitions past the first success beat switching after a single success ([Porter et al. via Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/three-different-types-of-practice-strategies-which-one-works-best/)). **[Proposal]** The plan's weekly rotation already suits a hybrid. Monday and Tuesday are for blocked reps on a new chunk. From Wednesday to Friday, once the chunk is reliable, the learner rotates every 3–5 minutes between the target chunk, its join and an older piece. The app should warn that this *feels* worse but tends to stick better.

Rest and sleep belong in the plan too. **[Evidence, moderate, non-music motor sequence]** Buch et al. found that most within-session gains in a typing sequence happened during 10-second rests, when the brain replayed the sequence ([Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/evidence-that-you-should-probably-be-taking-practice-micro-breaks-if-you-want-to-maximize-learning/)). **[Evidence, moderate]** A keyboard melody learned in the evening improved after sleep (Simmons & Duke 2006, via a secondary summary at [nkoda](https://www.nkoda.com/blog/Sleep)). But when evening practice was **cut short in time and repetitions**, performance was *worse* the next morning ([Allen & Duke 2013](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1019739)). That result supports two design choices: end on a stable, correct performance, and judge a new phrase the day after learning it. The plan's two-separate-days milestone rule already matches this. **[Evidence, moderate]** Mental practice alone helps less than physical practice, but adding it costs nothing ([Bernardi et al. 2013](https://online.ucpress.edu/mp/article-abstract/30/3/275/62568/Mental-Practice-in-Music-Memorization-An)). In a randomised within-subjects trial with 50 trumpet students, a combined **sing + imagine + play** strategy matched physical repetition and was the only one that significantly improved expression (p = 0.002) ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)). Attentional focus is contested. One study favoured external, sound-directed cues ([Mornell & Wulf 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429418801573)), but a 2024 systematic review of 15 music studies found **no consistent advantage** for either focus ([Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1290596/full)). Sound-based cues such as "melody rings over the thumb" are therefore a low-risk default, not a proven gain.

**[Proposal] Practice rules to add to `rules`/`scoring`:**

| Rule | Wording for the app | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Clean tempo | 3 consecutive reps with no stops, pulse held, relaxation scored 2 | Proposal informed by Duke 2009 |
| Tempo raise | +4–6 BPM (5–10%) after a clean tempo; at most about 10% per passage per day; on a miss, drop back to the last clean tempo | Proposal; no study fixes these numbers |
| Exploration rep | Once per session, one rep 10–15% faster; it does not count toward clean tempo | Proposal echoing tempo variation in Duke 2009 |
| Error rule | Stop at the first error, name it (fret, pick or count), change one variable, retry; never repeat an identical failed attempt | Duke 2009; Killion & Duke 2025 |
| Finish rule | End each chunk and the session with 2–3 correct reps at the clean tempo | Allen & Duke 2013 (inferred) |
| Micro-rest | One breath and a hand release every 3–5 reps | Buch 2021 (non-music) |
| Retention test | Tuesday and Sunday: play the week's phrase once, cold, before warming up, and log the result | Proposal |

Self-scoring needs an anchor. **[Evidence, moderate]** Audio and video playback both improve self-assessment accuracy, and audio is better for auditory elements ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-1525-7_21)). Self-ratings still drift from expert ratings, and listening to high-quality models helps ([Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1568021/pdf)). **[Proposal]** On Saturday, listen to the model recording first and score from playback rather than while playing. Compare the clip with last month's, not only last week's. Once a month, add an outside ear (a teacher, a friend or a forum) to supply the feedback Ericsson's definition requires. On motivation, **[Evidence, moderate–strong]** habit automaticity took a median of **about 66 days (range 18–254)**, and a single missed day made no material difference ([Lally et al. 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)). That supports keeping "no practice debt" and adding a **"never miss twice"** rule. The learner should expect practice to feel automatic around Month 3.

## Load, not talent, is the injury lever for a 50-minute adult

The risks a beginner can control are sudden jumps in load, long unbroken sessions, poor seating and a perfectionist mindset. **[Expert, BAPAM]** A sudden increase in playing load is a common trigger for injury. BAPAM lists the warning signs that need help: pain that returns despite rest, swelling, pins and needles, weakness, reduced dexterity and increased tension ([BAPAM Return to Play](https://www.bapam.org.uk/return-to-play/)). **[Evidence, moderate]** In the RISMUS survey of 850 music students, **48%** reported playing-related musculoskeletal disorders in the past year. High exertion after **45 minutes of practice without breaks** was a significant associated factor ([PMC7725387](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7725387/)). **[Evidence, weak–moderate]** Among amateur young guitarists, the neck and lower back were the most common pain sites (29% each), followed by the wrist (17%). Awkward sitting without back support was the main associated factor ([J Musculoskeletal Research 2013](https://www.worldscientific.com/doi/10.1142/S0218957713300020)). **[Evidence, moderate reviews]** Focal dystonia affects 2–10% of *professional* musicians, and guitarists are a higher-risk group. Risk factors include overuse, irregular or intense practice periods, sudden technique changes and perfectionism ([J Hand Therapy 2024](https://www.jhandtherapy.org/article/S0894-1130(24)00024-3/fulltext)). Dystonia patients also ruminate more about mistakes ([Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.882966/full)). Risk for an amateur playing 50 minutes a day is unknown but presumably very low. The plan's framing still matters, and its "small slips are acceptable" pass rule is already well aligned.

The plan's structure is mostly safe already. Its longest unbroken stretch is the 20-minute solo block after a 2-minute put-down. The gaps are in the first weeks and at technique transitions. **[Expert/commercial, weak]** Fingertip soreness typically lasts 1–2 weeks and calluses form over 2–4 weeks. Guides suggest 15–20 minute sessions early on, lighter strings and low action ([Yousician](https://yousician.com/blog/guitar-calluses); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/guitar-fingers)). **[Expert, luthier guides]** Typical steel-string action at the 12th fret is about 2.4 mm on the low E and 1.6 mm on the high E, and fingerstyle players often go slightly lower ([MusicNomad](https://musicnomadcare.com/guides/the-complete-guide-to-string-action-height/)). **[Expert, pedagogy]** Classical teachers suggest a short, smooth nail at the nail–flesh junction ([This is Classical Guitar](https://www.thisisclassicalguitar.com/fingernails-on-classical-guitar/)). Steel-string players weigh nails against flesh differently ([Adam Rafferty](https://www.adamrafferty.com/2012/03/19/guitar-right-hand-technique-nails-vs-flesh/)). Sungha Jung uses acrylic-strengthened nails, an idea he took from Oshio ([PlayFingerstyleGuitar](https://www.playfingerstyleguitar.com/how-to-play-like-sungha-jung)).

**[Proposal]** The researchers suggest six safeguards:

- **Setup check (Week 1).** Measure the action. If the low E is above about 3 mm, get a professional setup. Consider light strings (12s or custom-light 11s).
- **Fingertip ramp (Days 1–14).** Split the session (for example 25 + 25 minutes) or cap fretting-hand time, using the ear and mental-practice blocks as finger-off time.
- **New-technique load cap.** For the first week of any new technique (slap, barre, partial barre), cap it at **5–10 minutes a day**.
- **Posture check.** Use a chair with back support and a monthly posture/tension video.
- **Nail decision (around Month 2–3).** Nails are optional; if used, keep them short and smooth.
- **Warning-sign ladder in `rules`.** (a) Mild fingertip tenderness is normal. (b) Aching that clears within hours means shorten the session, add breaks and check posture. (c) Pain that persists to the next day or returns despite rest, or numbness, tingling, weakness, loss of dexterity or involuntary finger curling, means stop and see a clinician, ideally a performing-arts medicine one.

The log prompt should record one thing that improved rather than a list of errors. Evidence for particular warm-ups, seating styles, string gauges or callus timelines is essentially absent. These are expert defaults.

## Technique should follow the thumb-first, percussion-early path teachers use

Teaching sources agree on a sequence, and the plan follows it only loosely. **[Expert]** A beginner framework runs: posture, p-i-m-a assignment, free stroke, single-finger evenness, alternation, thumb independence, pinch, arpeggios, then variations. It adds rest stroke for melody emphasis once coordination is solid ([Guitar Wiz](https://guitarwiz.app/articles/fingerstyle-guitar-foundation/)). Jamie Stillway's Travis sequence builds alternating bass with **the thumb alone first** (root–fifth, root–third). Treble notes are then added as quarters, eighths and syncopations, then applied to a progression, then decorated with hammer-ons, and finally used to arrange melodies. She also mentions palm-muting the bass ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/lesson-learn-the-nuts-and-bolts-of-travis-picking/)). Adam Rafferty's three independence drills are: thumb moves while the fingers stay still; fingers move while the thumb stays still; sing the groove, then combine with attention on the bass ([Adam Rafferty](https://www.adamrafferty.com/2015/01/03/thumb-and-finger-independence-for-fingerstyle-guitarists/)). Tommy Emmanuel's first principle is that "the thumb plays a solid time" while the fingers play, worked slowly with a metronome ([Guitar World](https://www.guitarworld.com/lessons/tommy-emmanuels-guide-developing-hand-independence-and-more-el-vaquero-video)). Douglas Niedt teaches rest stroke specifically to bring a melody out of an arpeggio ([Niedt](https://douglasniedt.com/reststrokeformelodyofarpeggiopart1.html)). That is the most direct mechanical fix for the plan's Month 5 "melody buried" fault.

Against this, `curriculum.json` has specific gaps:

- The `alternating-thumb` exercise starts in Month 4 at 40–50 BPM quarters, with no earlier thumb-alone strand.
- None of the eight exercises covers pinch, monotonic bass, palm muting or rest stroke.
- Hammer-ons wait until Month 7, although Stillway places them inside the Travis sequence.
- The only harmonic is an optional fret-12, string-1 harmonic in Month 9.
- Full F stays "optional later" all year.
- Month 10 says to skip percussion.
- Month 12 re-tests only Pattern A.

The percussion omission matters most for this learner's goal. **[Expert]** Sungha Jung uses a thumb slap on beats 2 and 4 in "almost all" his songs, along with legato, barre chords, glissando, natural harmonics, and occasional tapping and alternate tunings ([PlayFingerstyleGuitar](https://www.playfingerstyleguitar.com/how-to-play-like-sungha-jung)). Oshio's signature "nail attack" strikes the strings with the middle- and ring-finger nails to keep a beat under the melody ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotaro_Oshio); [Acoustic Accent](https://acousticaccent.wordpress.com/2015/11/05/kotaro-oshio-fingerstyle-perfection-and-nail-attack/)). Teachers call the thumb slap one of the most intuitive percussive techniques for beginners ([BlitzGuitar](https://www.blitzguitar.com/the-most-simple-percussive-guitar-technique-for-beginners/)). Doug Young's graded percussion lesson begins with a slap on strings 3–5 on beats 2 and 4 against a metronome. It then adds chords, syncopation, sustained notes, and finally a full piece ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/learn-these-percussive-fingerstyle-guitar-techniques-to-add-punch-and-groove-to-your-playing/)). Harmonics have a similar graded path. Jeff Gunn's artificial-harmonic sequence starts with a single chimed note, and he says beginners should master fretting and chord shapes first ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/lesson-5-ways-to-master-artificial-harmonics-on-an-acoustic-guitar/)). Natural harmonics at frets 12, 7 and 5 have no such prerequisite. **[Expert]** JustinGuitar places barre chords at the end of its beginner grades, after easier partial barres ([JustinGuitar](https://www.justinguitar.com/modules/beginner-consolidation-easy-barre-chords)).

**[Proposal]** Year one should therefore include palm-muted bass, a backbeat slap, a thumb slap that replaces the bass on 2 and 4, natural harmonics at 12/7/5, a finger strum or flick that prepares for the nail attack, drop D, and one open tuning for a single excerpt. Year two should cover harp-harmonic cascades, the nail attack at tempo, tapping and frequent retuning. Published beginner lessons almost never give BPM targets. The one explicit figure is 50–60 BPM for foundation patterns, played where they feel "almost too easy" ([Guitar Wiz](https://guitarwiz.app/articles/fingerstyle-guitar-foundation/)). The benchmark tempos below are the researchers' own and deliberately modest. Many Jung and Oshio pieces run well above 100 BPM with sixteenth-note textures, so year-one excerpts should be played at a personal tempo.

| End of month | Proposed technique benchmark [Proposal] | Tempo |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pattern A over C–Am–Em–G, 16 bars; p+a pinch on each chord | 50–60 BPM quarters |
| 2 | Pattern B through changes; thumb-alone alternating bass on Am/C/Em, 8 bars | 55–65 BPM eighths |
| 3 | Monotonic eighth-note root bass under a 4-bar melody; palm-muted option | 60 BPM eighths |
| 4 | Travis p-i-p-m with one pinch per bar; one hammer-on in time | 60–70 BPM eighths |
| 5 | Travis steady; rest-stroke melody audibly above bass on recording | 70–80 BPM |
| 6 | Backbeat slap on 2 and 4, 16 bars without drift; harmonics at 12/7/5 on all strings | 70–80 BPM |
| 7–8 | Thumb slap replaces bass on 2 and 4 under a melody; vibrato; partial barre in a piece | 70–90 BPM |
| 9–10 | Drop-D or open-tuning excerpt of 4–8 bars; finger strum + backbeat groove | 70–90% of recording tempo |
| 12 | Three-piece set including one percussive piece; Travis eighths | 80–90 BPM |

## A graded ladder from Amazing Grace to a standard-tuning Oshio ballad

Nearly all of Oshio's well-known pieces are intermediate or harder, and most combine alternate tunings with slaps, tapping or harmonics. **[Expert, hobbyist/school sources]** A Japanese solo-guitar blog names **桜・咲くころ (Sakura Saku Koro)** and **木漏れ日 (Komorebi)** as beginner-accessible because they are in standard tuning ([akogimania](https://akogimania.com/beginner/etude)). A guitar school lists **風の彼方 (Kaze no Kanata)** as slow and attemptable ([TSG](https://tsg-gt.com/info/6217/)). Twilight (黄昏) is in standard tuning, but players rate it harder than it looks because of its controlled touch ([Yahoo Chiebukuro](https://detail.chiebukuro.yahoo.co.jp/qa/question_detail/q1138570661)). A fan tuning list puts Christmas Rose and Tension in CGDGBD, Merry Christmas Mr. Lawrence in DADGAC and Splash in DADGAD ([ishiyamania-g](https://ishiyamania-g.seesaa.net/category/7156342-1.html)). These attributions should be confirmed against the official score. For Jung, the recommended entry points are the earlier originals **Siesta and L'Atelier, then Tadhana**, avoiding *Racing Into The Night* ([PlayFingerstyleGuitar](https://www.playfingerstyleguitar.com/how-to-play-like-sungha-jung)).

Six String Fingerpicking provides a ready backbone. Its **1–8 difficulty scale runs from Novice to Expert across 500+ songs** ([SSF](https://sixstringfingerpicking.com/songs/)), and Amazing Grace is Level 1 with a free PDF ([SSF Amazing Grace](https://sixstringfingerpicking.com/songs/amazing-grace-guitar-lesson-easy/)). Nanzawa Daisuke's Ghibli books use fewer notes in standard or drop D tuning, and Kishibe Masaaki's slow pieces introduce open tunings with simple fingering. Both are natural bridges to Oshio ([akogimania](https://akogimania.com/beginner/etude)). **[Proposal]** The ladder below follows those sources. ## Hear in numbers, count on 2 and 4, memorise by landing spots

The plan's ear block is already melody-first and on the instrument. The Frontiers trumpet trial supports keeping singing tied to playing: singing alone gave no pitch advantage, while sing + imagine + play gave the best expression ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)). The gap is **functional hearing**: recognising a note as a scale degree in a key, which is exactly what arranging needs. **[Expert/tool]** Functional Ear Trainer plays a cadence to set the key, then asks you to name a note's scale degree ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/functional-ear-trainer/id1088761926)). **[Proposal]** Play the tonic chord before every hum-and-find, and grow the ear strand in stages:

- degrees 1-3-5 in Months 1–2;
- "home or not home" endings in Month 3;
- degrees 1–7 in C and G in Month 4;
- bass-root dictation over recordings in Month 5;
- 2–4-bar melody dictation in Months 6–7;
- hearing choruses as 1-4-5-6m in Month 8;
- a 4–8-bar Jung or Oshio transcription with a slow-down tool in Months 9–10.

Timing work should happen on the guitar. **[Evidence, moderate]** Musicians keep time better both with and without a metronome. String players also synchronise less variably on their own instrument than when finger-tapping, which suggests timing skill is specific to the trained movement ([Frontiers 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00069/full)). **[Expert]** Moving the click to beats 2 and 4, starting from half tempo, strengthens the internal pulse ([ClassicalGuitarReview](https://www.classicalguitarreview.com/practice-timing-with-metronome/2505/); [Guitar World](https://www.guitarworld.com/lessons/techniques/how-to-use-a-metronome-for-guitar)). No controlled study compares this with clicking on every beat. **[Proposal]** The progression is: count aloud in Months 1–2; lock the thumb to a click in Months 3–4; speak sixteenths in Month 5; click on 2 and 4, then "gap clicks", in Months 6–7; line the slap up with the 2-and-4 click in Months 8–9; play along with original recordings in Months 10–12. The click on 2 and 4 then does double duty as training for the backbeat.

For arranging, the Month 8 milestone needs a method, not just a goal. **[Expert]** The Nashville Number System writes chords as degrees. In any major key 1, 4 and 5 are major and 2, 3 and 6 are minor, so a chart transposes instantly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System)). Alex de Grassi finds the melody within the chords, isolates it as single-note phrases, then combines the two ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/tips-to-create-a-solo-fingerstyle-guitar-arrangement/)). Guitareo builds root–fifth bass first and places the melody on the higher strings ([GuitarLessons.com](https://www.guitarlessons.com/guitar-lessons/guitar-lessons-for-beginners/arranging-solo-fingerstyle-songs/)). Cameron Knowler links chords with scalar bass runs and uses syncopation sparingly ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/techniques-for-arranging-traditional-tunes-on-guitar/)). Tommy Emmanuel sings the melody first and studies how singers phrase it ([Guitar World](https://www.guitarworld.com/features/tommy-emmanuel-tips-for-acoustic-guitar)). Sungha Jung arranges largely by ear, mentored by Ulli Bögershausen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sungha_Jung)).

**[Proposal] Month 8 recipe:**

1. Sing the hook until it is secure.
2. Choose C, G or D so the melody sits on strings 1–3 below about fret 7.
3. Write chord numbers above each bar.
4. Play a root on beat 1, then root–fifth if the hand allows.
5. Keep the melody as the top note, dropping any chord tone above it.
6. Fill only where the melody holds a long note.
7. Build the intro and outro from the hook, ending on the 1 chord.

Month 9 then adds one colour device at a time: a bass run, a hammer-on, a slap on 2 and 4, or an add9/sus shape. Each is recorded plain and coloured for A/B comparison. **[Proposal]** TAB with rhythm stems plus chord numbers is sufficient notation for year one. No study compares TAB with staff notation for adult guitarists.

Memorisation for Month 11 should be built around cues, not motor chaining. **[Evidence, moderate]** Chaffin's research shows that reliable memorised performance depends on deliberately rehearsed **performance cues**, structural and expressive landmarks from which the player can restart. Serial chaining, where each phrase triggers the next, fails under nerves ([Bulletproof Musician on Chaffin](https://bulletproofmusician.com/musicians-seem-memorization-naturals-can-become-one/)). Novices rely on motor and auditory memory, whereas experts add a conceptual map ([Chaffin, Demos & Logan 2016](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2018/01/2016-Chaffin-Demos-Logan-Performing_from_Memory.pdf)). **[Expert]** Recovery advice is to keep the beat, avoid signalling the slip, and rehearse under mild induced arousal: recording, a distracting listener, or exercise before a run ([Strings Magazine](https://stringsmagazine.com/recovering-onstage-after-a-mistake-and-how-to-prepare-your-pieces-to-help-avoid-it/); [Musical U with Kageyama](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/becoming-a-bulletproof-musician-with-noa-kageyama/)). **[Evidence, moderate]** About three-quarters of young amateurs fall into a low-symptom, high-coping anxiety profile ([PMC10425269](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10425269/)). Where anxiety is a real problem, CBT, mindfulness and ACT interventions help ([PMC12062091](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12062091)). For Month 12's recording, **[Expert]** audio engineers aim the microphone at the 12th–14th fret from about 6–12 inches, away from the boomy soundhole ([Audient](https://audient.com/tutorial/5-essential-tips-for-recording-acoustic-guitar/)). One source suggests 12–18 inches for a phone ([Music Guy Mixing](https://www.musicguymixing.com/how-to-record-acoustic-guitar/)).

## Conclusion and month-by-month changes

The plan's biggest weakness is not effort or structure. It is that the style-defining skills of the target (a steady independent thumb, a backbeat under the melody, harmonics, barres and one open tuning) arrive late, arrive as options, or never arrive, while practice quality is left to intuition. Moving a small daily technique strand (5–10 of the 50 minutes, taken from the picking block) onto the thumb-first, percussion-early sequence costs no extra time. Codifying the clean-rep, finish-on-correct and interleaving rules turns the existing weekly rotation into something close to deliberate practice with feedback. The evidence is honest but thin: no guitar-specific trial validates any of the numbers, so the app should present them as adjustable rules of thumb. A monthly outside listener is the cheapest way to correct the drift that self-scoring introduces.

**Cross-cutting changes (all months) [Proposal]:** Add the practice-rules table above to `rules` and `scoring`. Add the warning-sign ladder, "never miss twice", and a 5–10 minute cap on any new technique for its first week. Use blocked practice Monday–Tuesday and interleaved Wednesday–Friday, with cold retention takes on Tuesday and Sunday. On Saturday, listen to the model first, then score from playback. Take a monthly posture video and get outside feedback once a month. Log "one thing that improved" each day.
