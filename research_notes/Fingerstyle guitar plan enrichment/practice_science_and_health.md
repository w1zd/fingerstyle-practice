# Practice Science and Player Health for an Adult Beginner's Daily 50-Minute Fingerstyle Routine

Context checked against the current plan (`app/curriculum.json`): the routine already has a 2-min tune, 8-min coordination, 10-min picking, a 2-min put-down break, a 20-min solo block (melody → bass → hardest 1–2 bars → longer section), 5-min ear, and a 3-min continuous take plus log. The weekly rotation runs Mon learn, Tue watch and apply, Wed join and balance, Thu extend, Fri repair, Sat record, Sun easy review. Scoring uses 0–2 across timing, clarity, continuity, balance and relaxation, and the monthly pass must be shown on two separate days. Rules include no practice debt and stop on pain, tingling or numbness.

Gaps I found in the plan: no explicit tempo-raise rule (for example, how many clean reps before adding BPM), no end-of-session "finish on a correct rep" rule, no interleaving between the solo-piece chunks and older material inside a session, no micro-rests inside the 20-min block, no external-focus cue language, no fingertip/callus ramp for month 1, no nail-care or guitar-setup check, and no warning-sign ladder beyond "stop if pain".

Evidence labels used below: **[Strong]** = replicated experiments or meta-analysis; **[Moderate]** = one or a few well-designed music studies; **[Weak/Expert]** = observational, small-N, pedagogy opinion or commercial sources.

## Q1. What does motor-learning research (musicians especially) say about blocked vs interleaved practice, slow practice and tempo ramps, chunking, spacing, sleep, mental practice, attentional focus, variability and error-based learning?

### Takeaway
For a beginner, block first until a chunk is reliably correct, then interleave. Use slow, musical, "made doable" repetitions, and raise tempo only after consecutive clean reps. Build in short rests. End each item on correct reps. Expect some gains to show up the next day. Music-specific evidence for most of these effects is modest and small-N, so treat them as good defaults, not laws.

### Cited Findings
**Blocked vs interleaved (contextual interference)**
- [Moderate] Carter & Grahn (2016) tested 10 advanced clarinetists on two concerto expositions and two études. Blocked practice was 12 continuous minutes per piece; interleaved practice alternated 3-minute segments (12 min per piece in total). Retention was tested the next day. Interleaved practice was rated better when averaged across three raters, but individual raters differed. Six of ten players found interleaving "more useful", and eight said it helped goal-setting, focus and spotting mistakes. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- [Moderate] Earlier music studies are inconsistent. Stambaugh (2009, 2011) found interleaving gave faster playing speeds in beginner and university woodwind players. Stambaugh & Demorest (2010) found no accuracy difference, and both blocked and interleaved beat a hybrid schedule. — as reviewed in [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- [Moderate] Interleaving hurts performance during practice but improves retention. Learners still prefer blocked practice and judge it more effective, even when interleaving produced more learning. — [Bulletproof Musician summary](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-misinterpreted-effort-hypothesis-and-why-we-often-practice-less-effectively-than-we-could/); [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- [Moderate, non-music] In novice basketball shooters (Porter et al., 2019; n=36; 6 sessions), blocked practice of 15 shots per location gave the best overall improvement and transfer. Switching after a single success ("learner-adapted") gave little gain on the practised shots. Kageyama's reading: beginners should do blocked reps past first success, then move toward variation as reliability grows. — [Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/three-different-types-of-practice-strategies-which-one-works-best/)
- [Moderate] A 2025 JRME study by Mathias & Goldman looked specifically at raising contextual interference within a music practice session, measuring acquisition and retention. I only had the title; results were not verified. — [JRME 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00224294231222801)

**Slow practice, tempo, "doable-izing" and error correction**
- [Moderate] Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009) observed 17 advanced pianists learning a 3-bar Shostakovich passage and tested retention the next day. Retention was not related to practice time, total trials or complete trials. The best performers found and fixed errors immediately, pinpointed their source, varied the tempo of problem segments systematically, and repeated targets until errors were gone. They played with the fewest inaccurate trials. — [JRME 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249808857_It's_Not_How_Much_It's_How_Characteristics_of_Practice_Behavior_and_Retention_of_Performance_Skills)
- [Moderate, small-N observational] Killion & Duke (2025, *Journal of Expertise*) analysed six professional trumpeters repetition by repetition. They shared one "central strategy":
  - a clear intention before each attempt, with about 43% of time spent thinking rather than playing;
  - changing something after a failed attempt instead of repeating it identically;
  - "doable-izing" the passage (fewer notes, slower, softer) so it can be played correctly;
  - keeping expression even in simplified reps;
  - adding complexity back gradually while keeping a high success rate;
  - reflective pauses between attempts.
  — [Bulletproof Musician summary](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-central-strategy-expert-musicians-use-to-practice-difficult-passages/)
- [Weak/Expert, survey] Allingham & Wöllner (2022) surveyed 256 adult instrumentalists. Slow practice is almost universal (99.45% of classical and 89.12% of non-classical players). It serves technical, expressive and preparatory functions. A 2023 follow-up interview study covers perceived limits of slow practice. — [Psychology of Music 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03057356211073481); [Psychology of Music 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03057356221129650)
- [Moderate, correlational] Miksza (2007) found that high-school wind players who used more repetition of sections, whole-part-whole and slowing had higher performance achievement. — [JRME 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429408317513)
- [Moderate, correlational] A 2015 Miksza intervention with collegiate wind players compared instruction in practice strategies (slowing, repetition, whole-part-whole, chaining) with instruction in self-regulation (concentration, goal-selection, planning, self-evaluation, rest/reflection). — [Psychology of Music 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735613500832) (detailed results not retrieved)

**Rest, spacing and consolidation**
- [Moderate, non-music but motor-sequence] Buch et al. (2021) had 33 participants type a 5-digit sequence in 10-second blocks with 10-second rests. Most of the improvement happened during the rests, when the brain "replayed" the sequence about 20 times faster than real performance. Kageyama recommends:
  - a few reps;
  - one conscious breath as a micro-break;
  - a test rep;
  - repeat the cycle.
  Micro-breaks also relieve build-up of muscle tension. — [Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/evidence-that-you-should-probably-be-taking-practice-micro-breaks-if-you-want-to-maximize-learning/)
- [Moderate] Simmons & Duke (2006): people who learned a keyboard melody in the evening and were tested after sleep improved markedly. Those who learned it in the morning and were tested that evening did not. — reported in [nkoda blog](https://www.nkoda.com/blog/Sleep) and [Training the Musical Brain](http://trainingthemusicalbrain.blogspot.com/2011/10/sleep-and-memory-consolidation.html). These are secondary sources. The primary study is Simmons & Duke, *JRME* 2006, which I did not retrieve.
- [Moderate, important caveat] Allen & Duke (2013) had 32 musicians practise a piano melody in the evening, but practice was **limited in time and number of repetitions**. They performed *worse* the next morning, not better. The authors think the restricted practice may explain why there was no overnight gain. — [Update 2013 / ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1019739); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/8755123313502350)
- [Moderate] Cash, Allen, Simmons & Duke (2014) studied how hearing model performances affects skill acquisition and overnight consolidation. — [JRME 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429413520409) (abstract only; details not retrieved)
- [Weak] One blog says that learning a similar task 4–6 hours after practice interferes with consolidation. I could not trace this to a primary study, so I would not use it. — [nkoda](https://www.nkoda.com/blog/Sleep)

**Mental practice**
- [Moderate] Bernardi et al. (2013) had 16 pianists memorise pieces. Mental practice alone produced gains, but smaller than physical practice. Mental plus physical practice was indistinguishable from physical practice alone. Pitch imagery and structural analysis predicted better results after mental practice. — [Music Perception 2013](https://online.ucpress.edu/mp/article-abstract/30/3/275/62568/Mental-Practice-in-Music-Memorization-An); [summary in Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)
- [Moderate] A 2021 Frontiers study, "Imagine, Sing, Play", reports that combining mental, vocal and physical practice improves musical performance. — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full) (title/abstract level)

**Attentional focus**
- [Moderate] Mornell & Wulf (2019) found that an external focus, on the intended sound or effect, improved expression in two experiments, and improved technical precision compared with an internal focus on the body. — [JRME 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429418801573)
- [Weak–Moderate: conflicting] A 2024 systematic review (15 studies, 401 participants, 63 different outcome measures) found **no consistent superiority** of external or internal focus in music. Sample sizes were small and methods varied. The authors call for a music-specific focus model. External cues used in studies included "focus on the sound you produce" and aiming the sound at a chair about 24 ft away. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1290596/full)

**Variability**
- [Weak/Expert] Tempo variation (slowing down, then speeding up) is presented as a form of variable practice that helps avoid mindless automatic repetition. — [Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-misinterpreted-effort-hypothesis-and-why-we-often-practice-less-effectively-than-we-could/). Duke et al. 2009 also observed systematic tempo variation in the best practicers. — [JRME 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)

### Inferences (concrete prescriptions for this plan)
- **Tempo rule (proposed):** first make the chunk doable, slowing it and thinning it to one voice if needed.
  - Raise the metronome by about 4–6 BPM, or 5–10%, only after **3 consecutive clean, relaxed reps**. On a miss, go back to the last clean tempo; do not push through.
  - Once a day, try one rep ~10–15% above the clean tempo as an exploration, then return to the clean tempo. This echoes the tempo variation seen in Duke 2009.
  - The exact numbers are my synthesis. No study fixes "3 reps" or "5 BPM".
- **Error rule:** stop at the first error and name its source (fretting, picking or count). Change one variable, then retry. Never repeat an identical failed attempt (Duke 2009; Killion & Duke 2025).
- **Finish rule:** end each target chunk, and the session, on 2–3 correct reps at the clean tempo. Allen & Duke 2013 suggests that cutting practice short before performance is stable may block overnight gains.
- **Blocked → interleaved:** in the 20-min solo block, use blocked reps on a *new* chunk (Mon/Tue), since beginners benefit from more reps past first success (Porter 2019).
  - Once a chunk is reliable (Wed–Fri), rotate every 3–5 minutes between 2–3 items: the target chunk, the join and an older piece. This follows the 3-minute segments in Carter & Grahn.
  - Warn the learner in the app copy that interleaving *feels* worse but tends to stick better.
- **Micro-rests:** in the 8-min "hardest bars" segment, add a breath-length pause and a brief hand release after every 3–5 reps (Buch 2021 via Kageyama). This also serves injury prevention.
- **Thinking time:** allow a few seconds before each rep to hear the target mentally (Killion & Duke: about 43% of time not playing). The plan should not measure success by constant playing.
- **Mental practice:** use it as a supplement, not a replacement: humming, the ear block, or imagining the phrase away from the guitar on minimum days or when fingers are sore. Combining it with physical practice brings no penalty (Bernardi 2013).
- **Focus cues:** default to sound-based cues ("make the melody note ring over the thumb", "keep the bass even like a clock"). Keep a body cue only when something is wrong, for example tension. The evidence for external focus in music is mixed, so this is a low-risk default rather than a proven gain.
- **Sleep:** do not judge a new phrase on the day it is learned. The Monday phrase should be judged on Tuesday. This supports the plan's existing two-separate-days milestone rule.

### Gaps
- There are no guitar-specific randomised trials of interleaving, tempo ramps or focus of attention. I found none; all findings are borrowed from piano, wind, brass or non-music tasks.
- No study I found gives an evidence-based number of clean reps before raising tempo, or a BPM increment. The rule above is expert synthesis.
- I did not retrieve the Simmons & Duke 2006 primary paper or the Mathias & Goldman 2025 results.
- Simmons, Allen, Cash & Duke (2019), "Effects of early break intervals on musicians' and nonmusicians' skill learning", is directly relevant to break timing, but the full text was blocked (403), so I cannot report its findings. — [Psychology of Music 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735617735373)
- I found no evidence on chunk size specifically for guitarists. The plan's 1–2 bars matches common practice but is not evidence-derived.

## Q2. What is the evidence on session length and breaks, deliberate practice vs mere repetition (Ericsson and critiques), and strategy studies of musicians?

### Takeaway
How you practise predicts retention better than how long you practise. The amount of deliberate practice explains a meaningful but minority share of skill differences in music. A daily ~50-minute session split by short breaks fits the evidence. There is no strong evidence for an exact optimal session length.

### Cited Findings
- [Strong, meta-analysis] Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014) found that deliberate practice explained about 21% of performance variance in music: 26% in games, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. — [Psychological Science 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810)
- [Expert rebuttal] Ericsson & Harwell (2019) argue that the meta-analysis used loose definitions of deliberate practice. Under stricter criteria, the variance explained changes; one reanalysis cites 14% under restrictive criteria, which illustrates how much definitions matter. They argue the original definition requires teacher-designed, individualised practice with feedback. — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)
- [Moderate] In Duke et al. 2009, practice time and number of trials did not predict next-day retention; strategy did. — [JRME 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- [Moderate] In the RISMUS study of 850 European music students, high perceived exertion after 45 minutes of practice without breaks was associated with playing-related musculoskeletal disorders. — [RISMUS, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7725387/)
- [Moderate] Micro-rests of about 10 seconds in motor-sequence learning drive much of the within-session gain (Buch 2021). — [Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/evidence-that-you-should-probably-be-taking-practice-micro-breaks-if-you-want-to-maximize-learning/)
- [Moderate] Self-regulation research (Miksza) connects goal selection, planning, self-evaluation and rest/reflection to effective practice. — [Psychology of Music 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735613500832); [Miksza et al. 2012 observational](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429412455201)

### Inferences
- The plan's 50 minutes with a break at about minute 20 is sensible. The 20-min solo block that follows is the longest unbroken stretch. Given the RISMUS "45 minutes without breaks" association, keep the total below 45 minutes of continuous playing, which it already is, and add micro-rests within the solo block.
- Deliberate practice needs feedback. With no teacher, the recording and log, plus a monthly review of clips (ideally by a teacher or online feedback), stand in for the "teacher" part of Ericsson's definition.
- Set app expectations honestly: practice quality matters most, but individual differences are real, so progress will vary.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study comparing, for example, 1×50 min with 2×25 min daily practice for adult guitar beginners.
- I did not verify primary data on Ericsson's claims about daily practice limits (about 4 hours for experts); those claims are not relevant at 50 min/day in any case.

## Q3. How should self-recording, feedback and self-assessment be used?

### Takeaway
Record and listen back with a short rubric. Self-assessment accuracy improves with playback and with maturity, but self-ratings tend to disagree with expert ratings. Pair ratings with a model recording and occasional outside feedback.

### Cited Findings
- [Moderate] In tertiary instrument students, both audio and video recording improved self-assessment accuracy. Video improved overall accuracy but was slightly less accurate than audio for auditory elements. — [Springer chapter 2025](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-1525-7_21)
- [Moderate] Silveira & Gavin (2016) studied audio recording and playback for middle-school instrumentalists' self-assessment. — [Psychology of Music 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735615596375) (abstract only)
- [Weak–Moderate] Students' self-evaluations agree less with expert judgments. Accuracy rises with age and maturity. Self-evaluation combined with listening to high-quality models improves tone, rhythm, interpretation and overall performance. — [Frontiers 2025 PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1568021/pdf) (search-snippet level)
- [Moderate] Cash et al. (2014) examined how model performances affect skill acquisition and overnight consolidation. — [JRME 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429413520409)
- [Moderate] Hamilton & Duke (2026, JRME) study how well knowledgeable listeners can assess practice from audio recordings alone. — [JRME 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/00224294251407683) (title only)

### Inferences
- The plan's Saturday clip and 0–2 rubric fit this evidence. Suggested improvements:
  - Listen to the model recording (Oshio or Jung, or the lesson) *just before* self-scoring, as an anchor.
  - Score from audio rather than while playing.
  - Compare this month's clip with last month's clip, not only with this week's.
- Add a monthly "outside ear" step (teacher, friend or forum) for the milestone, since self-ratings drift from expert ratings.
- Use video monthly only for posture and tension checks. Audio is better for judging sound.

### Gaps
- No guitar-specific self-assessment studies were found.
- The newest studies (Springer 2025, Hamilton & Duke 2026) were only seen at abstract or title level.

## Q4. Plateaus and motivation for adult learners: goal setting, habit formation, missed days

### Takeaway
Habits take weeks to months to become automatic (about 66 days on average, with wide variation). Missing a single day does not materially set back habit formation. Stable cues, small process goals and "no practice debt" rules are well supported.

### Cited Findings
- [Moderate–Strong] Lally et al. (2010): median time to habit automaticity was about 66 days, with a range of 18–254 days. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. — [Eur J Soc Psychol 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674); [University of Surrey interview with Lally](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- [Moderate] Self-regulation training covering goal selection, planning, self-evaluation and rest is part of effective musical practice, and setting achievable goals and noticing individual progress is emphasised. — [Miksza 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735613500832)
- [Moderate] Performance during practice and actual learning differ: gains may disappear by the next day and reappear later. This is a documented source of perceived "plateaus". — [Bulletproof Musician, learning–performance distinction](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-learning-performance-distinction-and-why-gains-in-the-practice-room-often-dont-stick/)

### Inferences
- Keep the plan's "no practice debt" and 15-min minimum day. Lally supports this: one miss does not matter, but repeated misses erode the habit. Suggest "never miss twice" as the app rule.
- Tie practice to a fixed daily cue (same time and place, guitar left on a stand). Expect the habit to feel automatic around months 2–3 (Lally's ~66 days, falling around month 3 of 28-day months).
- For plateaus: compare monthly clips (objective progress), switch to an interleaved or older-piece day, and repeat a week as the plan already allows. Do not add session length.

### Gaps
- I found no adult-guitar-specific motivation or dropout research in this pass. Adult music-learning dropout statistics were not retrieved.

## Q5. Injury prevention: focal dystonia, tendonitis/RSI, calluses, warm-ups, posture, seating, guitar setup and nails

### Takeaway
The main controllable risks are:
- sudden increases in playing load;
- long sessions without breaks;
- awkward posture without back support;
- excess tension and perfectionist rumination (for dystonia).

Stop and seek help for pain that persists or recurs, numbness, tingling, weakness, loss of control or involuntary curling of a finger. Evidence on calluses, nails and setup is almost entirely expert or commercial opinion.

### Cited Findings
**Focal dystonia**
- [Moderate, reviews] Focal dystonia affects 2–10% of *professional* musicians, and guitarists are among the higher-risk groups. It appears more often in the more heavily used hand. — [J Hand Therapy review 2024](https://www.jhandtherapy.org/article/S0894-1130(24)00024-3/fulltext); [Musician's dystonia perspective, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11794226)
- [Moderate] Proposed risk factors:
  - genetic predisposition, previous injury and perfectionism;
  - excessive practice, repetitive movement and lack of pauses;
  - irregular or intense practice periods;
  - sudden changes in instrument or technique;
  - overload and emotional stress.
  — [J Hand Therapy review 2024](https://www.jhandtherapy.org/article/S0894-1130(24)00024-3/fulltext); [PubMed 2010 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20590806/)
- [Moderate, case-control] Musicians with dystonia showed more rumination about performance mistakes, more perfectionism and more authoritarian teaching, and had lower health support. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.882966/full)

**Musculoskeletal disorders (PRMDs), tendonitis/RSI**
- [Weak–Moderate] In amateur young guitarists, the neck and lower back were the most common pain sites (29% each), followed by the wrist (17%) and shoulder (15.5%). Awkward sitting posture and not using back support were the main factors. — [J Musculoskeletal Research 2013](https://www.worldscientific.com/doi/10.1142/S0218957713300020)
- [Moderate] In RISMUS (850 music students), 48% reported PRMDs in the past 12 months. High exertion after 45 minutes of practice without breaks was a significant associated factor. — [PMC7725387](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7725387/)
- [Moderate] A systematic review covers prevalence, risk factors and treatment effects of MSK disorders in professional musicians. — [Int Arch Occup Environ Health 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00420-019-01467-8)
- [Expert body – BAPAM] A common trigger for injury is a *sudden increase in playing load*. Pushing technique without guidance or adaptation time also causes injury. BAPAM advises seeking help for:
  - pain, weakness or swelling;
  - pins and needles;
  - excessive fatigue;
  - reduced dexterity;
  - increased muscle tension;
  - reduced range of movement;
  - pain that returns despite rest.
  It says to keep up warm-ups and breaks even when busy. — [BAPAM Return to Play](https://www.bapam.org.uk/return-to-play/); [BAPAM Fit to Play factsheet](https://music.fsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/FittoPlay_BAPAMfactsheet.pdf)

**Calluses and ramp-up**
- [Weak/commercial] Beginners typically have fingertip soreness for 1–2 weeks. Calluses form over about 2–4 weeks of consistent playing. Suggested sessions are 15–20 minutes a day early on to avoid blistering. Lighter-gauge strings and low action reduce pressure. Press only as hard as needed, and do not pick at calluses. — [Yousician](https://yousician.com/blog/guitar-calluses); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/guitar-fingers); [Aeroband](https://www.aeroband.net/blogs/blog/guitar-finger-pain-tips)

**Guitar setup**
- [Weak/Expert, luthier and retailer guides] Typical steel-string acoustic action at the 12th fret is about 2.4 mm (6/64") on the low E and about 1.6 mm (4/64") on the high E. Other guides give 2.5–3.0 mm on the low E and 2.0–2.5 mm on the high E. Fingerstyle players often prefer slightly lower action. Measure in tune, from the top of the fret to the bottom of the string. — [MusicNomad](https://musicnomadcare.com/guides/the-complete-guide-to-string-action-height/); [Stringjoy](https://stringjoy.com/guitar-action-the-complete-guide-to-string-height-action-adjustments/); [owningafender](https://owningafender.com/acoustic/setup/action-height)

**Nails vs flesh**
- [Expert/pedagogy] Most elite classical players use nails. Many teachers recommend contact at the nail–flesh junction to combine projection with warmth. A nail about 2–3 mm (1/8") past the fingertip is suggested, shaped smooth so the string does not snag; snags produce poor tone. — [This is Classical Guitar](https://www.thisisclassicalguitar.com/fingernails-on-classical-guitar/); [tonebase](https://www.tonebase.co/guitar-blog-posts/classical-guitar-nails); [Classical Guitar Shed](https://classicalguitarshed.com/classical-guitar-nails/)
- [Expert] Steel-string fingerstylist Adam Rafferty discusses the nails vs flesh trade-off for steel-string fingerstyle. — [Adam Rafferty](https://www.adamrafferty.com/2012/03/19/guitar-right-hand-technique-nails-vs-flesh/)

### Inferences
- **Month-1 fingertip ramp:** during the first 2 weeks, run the 50 minutes as two sessions (for example 25 + 25) or cap fretting-hand time. Treat the ear, mental-practice and listening blocks as "finger-off" time. Stop a session early if fingertips blister. After that, the full 50 minutes is fine.
- **Load changes:** avoid jumps in load. Do not double up after missed days (already in the plan). When a harder technique arrives, such as percussive slaps, barre or the Oshio-style thumb, cap it at about 5–10 minutes a day for the first week (BAPAM's "sudden increase" warning).
- **Warm-up:** the 2-min tune plus 8-min easy coordination block effectively acts as a warm-up. Keep its first 2–3 minutes deliberately light: slow open-chord changes, soft touch. Evidence for specific warm-up routines is lacking.
- **Posture and seating:** use a chair with back support. Try a footstool or a support device (classical position) versus casual right-leg seating, and choose whichever keeps the wrists neutral and the shoulders level. Check monthly on video. Evidence for one seating style over another was not found.
- **Setup:** add a one-time "setup check" task in week 1 (measure action; consider light 12s or custom-light 11s on steel string, and a pro setup if the low E is above about 3 mm at the 12th fret). Light strings help calluses and effort. The trade-off in volume and tone is minor for a beginner.
- **Nails:** the Oshio/Jung style is steel-string and percussive. Many players use short nails or flesh, sometimes with acrylic or gel reinforcement, because steel strings wear nails down fast. The app should present nails as optional, suggest a short smooth nail filed with fine-grit paper, and never require it. The claim about acrylic use is from my background knowledge and is not sourced here.
- **Dystonia-aware coaching language:** avoid perfectionist framing ("small slips are acceptable" is already in the pass rule, which is good). Log one improvement rather than rehearsing errors mentally. Avoid sudden changes in technique. Seek a specialist for painless loss of control or finger curling in one specific pattern.
- **Warning-sign ladder for the app:**
  - (a) Mild fingertip tenderness: normal; continue.
  - (b) Aching in the hand, forearm, wrist, neck or shoulder that goes away within hours: shorten sessions, add breaks, check posture and tension.
  - (c) Pain during playing that persists into the next day, or returns despite rest; swelling; numbness or tingling; weakness; loss of dexterity or involuntary finger movement: stop and consult a health professional, ideally a performing-arts medicine clinician (BAPAM list).

### Gaps
- There are no controlled studies on callus ramp-up, optimal string gauge or action for beginners. Sources are commercial or luthier guides.
- I found no peer-reviewed evidence on nail strengthening products or supplements for guitarists (biotin and similar). This topic was not researched in depth.
- I found no evidence comparing classical seating with casual seating for injury risk. A PLOS One study on posture and MSK complaints appeared in the search but was not read. — [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=printable&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0262207)
- Scott Tennant's *Pumping Nylon* and Jamie Andreas's *Principles of Correct Practice* were not retrieved. Their recommendations on relaxation, slow practice and posture are well known but are not cited here.
- Dystonia prevalence in amateur adult beginners is unknown. The 2–10% figure is for professionals, and risk at 50 min/day is likely very low (inference).

### Cited Findings
- Strategy matters more than time. Best practicers correct errors immediately, vary tempo and repeat until correct. — [Duke et al. 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)
- Interleave in 3-minute segments once skills are reliable. — [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/full)
- Beginners: blocked reps beyond first success. — [Porter et al. 2019 via Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/three-different-types-of-practice-strategies-which-one-works-best/)
- Micro-rests. — [Buch 2021 via Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/evidence-that-you-should-probably-be-taking-practice-micro-breaks-if-you-want-to-maximize-learning/)
- Make it doable, keep it musical, add complexity back gradually, think between reps. — [Killion & Duke 2025 via Bulletproof Musician](https://bulletproofmusician.com/the-central-strategy-expert-musicians-use-to-practice-difficult-passages/)
- Load spikes and 45+ minutes without breaks are associated with injury. — [BAPAM](https://www.bapam.org.uk/return-to-play/); [RISMUS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7725387/)
- Missing one day is harmless for habit formation. — [Lally 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674)

### Inferences (proposed changes; my synthesis)
**Daily session (50 min)**
1. **Tune and settle (2 min):** add a shoulder and hand release, plus "one sound goal for today".
2. **Coordination (8 min):** the first 2 minutes are an easy warm-up at a soft touch. After that, blocked reps on today's change with a breath-pause every 3–5 reps.
3. **Picking (10 min):** apply the tempo rule:
   - Start at the last logged clean tempo, with 1–2 reps slower as a reset.
   - Add 4–6 BPM after 3 consecutive clean, relaxed reps.
   - On any error, stop, name the cause, simplify, and drop back to the last clean tempo.
   - End with 2 correct reps.
4. **Put the guitar down (2 min):** keep this; add mental replay of the target phrase (hum or imagine it).
5. **Solo (20 min):**
   - Mon–Tue (new chunk): mostly blocked, going melody, then bass, then the 1–2 bar combination. Use micro-rests.
   - Wed–Fri: rotate every 3–5 minutes among the target chunk, its join, and an older piece or passage (interleaved).
   - Always keep one musical intention per rep, such as "melody note louder than the bass".
   - Finish the block with 2–3 correct reps at the clean tempo.
6. **Ear (5 min):** keep it. It also gives the fretting hand a rest.
7. **Continuous take and log (3 min):** log the clean tempo, the error type (fretting, picking or count) and tomorrow's single task. Log one thing that improved rather than a list of errors (dystonia-aware framing).

**Tempo rules to add to `scoring` or `rules`**
- "Clean tempo" means 3 consecutive reps without stops, with pulse held and hands relaxed (relaxation dimension at 2).
- Raise the tempo by at most about 5–10% per day on any passage.
- Once per session, try a rep 10–15% faster as an exploration. It does not count as the clean tempo.
- Judge a new tempo the next day, not the same day (consolidation).

**Weekly cycle**
- Monday (learn): blocked.
- Tuesday: check whether Monday's phrase "held overnight". Start by playing it cold once before any practice, and log the result as a retention test.
- Wednesday–Friday: interleaved schedule.
- Saturday (record): listen to the model recording before scoring, and score from playback.
- Sunday (easy review): keep it. Add one cold retention take of the week's phrase, played once and not repeated.

**Monthly and review**
- Keep the two-separate-days milestone (it matches the learning vs performance distinction).
- Add a monthly video of posture and tension, and optional outside feedback.
- Add a "load-change" flag when a new technique is introduced, capping it at 5–10 min a day for the first week.

**Month 1 add-ons**
- Setup check: action at the 12th fret, light strings.
- Split sessions or cap fretting time during days 1–14 for callus build-up.
- Nail decision (flesh or short, smooth nails) around month 2–3, when arpeggio patterns begin.

**Rules additions**
- Adopt a "never miss twice" rule.
- Add the warning-sign ladder (Q5).
- Stop for numbness, tingling, weakness, pain that persists into the next day, or involuntary curling.

### Gaps
- The specific numbers (3 clean reps, 4–6 BPM, 5–10%/day, 3–5 min interleave windows) are informed by the studies above but have not been validated for guitarists. They should be labelled "rule of thumb" in the app.
