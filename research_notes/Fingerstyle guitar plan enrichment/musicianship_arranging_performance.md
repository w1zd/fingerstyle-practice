# Musicianship, Arranging and Performance for a 12-Month Adult Fingerstyle Plan

Scope: ear training, rhythm/timing, applied theory and arranging, memorisation, performance and recording, reading. Target: slot into the existing daily 5-minute "listen, learn & apply" ear block plus the arranging months (8–10), set prep (11) and recording (12).

Current plan state (read from `app/curriculum.json`, `monthlyPlans[].ear` and `weeklyRotation[].ear`): M1 hum 3–5 notes and find them on one string; M2 find a short melody by ear, name bass roots of C/Am/Em/G; M3 mark phrase breaths, hum an ending; M4 locate C–B in one position, root/3rd/5th of C; M5 separate melody/bass/fill by ear, hum while tapping the pulse; M6 compare M1 vs now; M7 ornament with/without; M8 name root/3rd/5th under each phrase; M9 listen to Jung/Oshio three times (melody, bass, rhythm); M10 hum and count a dream-piece motif; M11 find weakest moment in a set take; M12 compare recordings. Weekly rotation: Mon hum/find, Tue lesson question, Wed diagnose a pause in a recording, Thu month's ear/theory task, Fri review/hum. The existing content is already "on-instrument, melody-first"; the gaps are (a) no key/scale-degree (functional) hearing, (b) no explicit metronome-off / backbeat pulse work, (c) no Nashville-number / diatonic-chord framework before the Month 8 arrangement, (d) no memory-cue ("landing spot") method before Month 11.

## Ear training: singing, audiation, dictation on the instrument, tools, and a practical progression

### Takeaway
The best evidence supports combining singing, mental imagery (audiation) and physical playing rather than replacing playing; functional (scale-degree-in-a-key) ear training maps directly onto arranging, and the plan's existing "hum then find on one string" approach is sound and should be extended into scale degrees and chord-root hearing by Month 4–8.

### Cited Findings
- Randomised within-subjects study of 50 academy trumpet students: five strategies (3x physical, 3x mental imagery, 3x singing/solfège, combined = one rep each of sing + play + imagine, and no practice). Combined and physical practice both improved overall performance vs no practice (p = 0.020, p = 0.019) and pitch accuracy (p = 0.015, p = 0.004); singing alone gave no pitch advantage; only the combined strategy improved musical expression significantly more than all others (p = 0.002). Prior solfège training correlated with better pitch improvement. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021, "Imagine, Sing, Play"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full) (also [PMC8573425](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8573425/))
- The authors conclude a mixed sing/imagine/play strategy was as efficient as extensive repetitive physical practice while giving better expression gains, reducing physical load. — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)
- There is a research literature on singing in instrumental instruction (review by H. Christian Bernhard, 2003, in *Update: Applications of Research in Music Education*), including studies of structured singing with beginning band students and effects on performance, tonal imagery and self-evaluation. — [Bernhard 2003, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/87551233020220010501); [ResearchGate: Singing in Instrumental Music Instruction](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341513515_Singing_in_Instrumental_Music_Instruction)
- Tommy Emmanuel's arranging approach centres on the melody; he recommends singing the melody and then capturing on guitar what you sang, and he listens to great singers (e.g., Ella Fitzgerald, Sinatra) to hear how they phrase a song before arranging it. — [Guitar World interview](https://www.guitarworld.com/features/tommy-emmanuel-tips-for-acoustic-guitar) / [Yahoo syndication: "I'm always true to the melody"](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/m-always-true-melody-tommy-091500549.html)
- Sungha Jung learns and arranges covers by ear and/or by watching videos; reported as roughly an hour to work out notes and fingering, about five more hours practising before recording, sometimes days to a month for accuracy. He was mentored in composing/arranging by Ulli Bögershausen. — [Wikipedia: Sungha Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sungha_Jung) (secondary; see also [New Age Parents interview](https://thenewageparents.com/sungha-jung-interview/))
- Functional Ear Trainer (Alain Benbassat method): each question plays a cadence (I–IV–V–I) to establish the key, then a tone; the user names its scale degree. The method trains recognising a note's function in a key (e.g., "3rd of C major") rather than isolated intervals; built for short daily sessions; iOS, Android and PC. — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/functional-ear-trainer/id1088761926); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.kaizen9.fet.android&hl=en_US); [AdvancingMusician overview](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training/)

### Inferences
- The 5-minute block should keep "sing/hum then find" as its core but add a key context: play the tonic chord first (e.g., C or G), sing the phrase, then find it. This is the on-instrument equivalent of the Functional Ear Trainer's cadence-then-tone design.
- Suggested progression (5 min/day, two sub-blocks of ~2.5 min):
  - M1–2: hum 3–5 notes, find on one string (as now). Add 1 min of Functional Ear Trainer levels 1–2 (degrees 1, 3, 5 in C) on 2–3 days a week.
  - M3: sing the phrase's last note and identify it as "home" (1) or "not home"; learn that most melodies end on 1.
  - M4: scale degrees 1–7 in C and G in one position; sing-and-play the C major scale with numbers (1-2-3...). Add FET degrees 1–5.
  - M5: bass-root dictation. Over a recording, hum the lowest note of each chord and find it on strings 6/5/4. This is the ear skill the Month 8 arrangement needs.
  - M6–7: 2–4-bar melody dictation of a familiar tune (Happy Birthday, a nursery tune, a pop chorus hook) by ear in C or G, written as TAB with numbers under it.
  - M8: hear chord changes as numbers (1, 4, 5, 6m) in a simple pop chorus; this directly supports the arrangement.
  - M9–10: short transcription (4–8 bars) of a Jung/Oshio or pop hook; slow down with an app; check against a tab only after trying.
  - M11–12: audiation (imagine the piece without playing) as memory check; sing the melody over the recording to judge whether it projects.
- Singing alone is not a substitute for playing (Frontiers 2021); treat humming as a bridge into the fingers, always followed by playing.

### Gaps

## Rhythm and timing: subdivision, counting, metronome on 2 and 4, groove, internal pulse

### Takeaway
Research shows musicians time more accurately with and without a metronome and that timing skill is partly instrument-specific, so pulse work should be done on the guitar, not just by tapping; pedagogues recommend moving the click to 2 and 4 (and then less often) to build internal time, which suits the backbeat slap of Oshio/Jung-style percussion.

### Cited Findings
- Musicians show greater accuracy and lower variability than non-musicians in both paced (with metronome) and unpaced (continuation, no metronome) tapping. — [Frontiers 2016, instrument-specific training and rhythm](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00069/full)
- String players synchronise with less variability when playing their own instrument than when finger tapping, suggesting timing advantages are specific to the trained movement. — [Frontiers 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00069/full)
- Tapping to a metronome may mainly use beat-maintenance processes, whereas tapping to music relies more on beat induction (finding the beat in complex sound). — [PMC3653057, emergent vs event-based timing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3653057/) (search summary; see also [ResearchGate: SMS and perception of timing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41027876_Sensorimotor_synchronization_and_perception_of_timing_Effects_of_music_training_and_task_experience))
- Synchronised metronome training has been shown to improve timing in non-musicians (soccer players). — [PMC6292953](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6292953/)
- Pedagogical recommendation: clicks on 2 and 4 force you to feel 1 and 3 yourself, strengthening the internal clock; start by setting the metronome to half tempo and treating clicks as the backbeat. — [ClassicalGuitarReview: practising timing with a metronome](https://www.classicalguitarreview.com/practice-timing-with-metronome/2505/); [Guitar World: how to use a metronome](https://www.guitarworld.com/lessons/techniques/how-to-use-a-metronome-for-guitar); [TrueFire: metronome time signatures](https://learn.truefire.com/metronome-time-signature)
- Apps exist that silence random or scheduled bars to test internal time (e.g., Time Trainer Metronome). — [App Store: Time Trainer](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/id502491350)

### Inferences
- Progression (fits into the 5-minute block or the start of the picking block):
  - M1–2: count aloud "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &" while playing bass-only; 3/4 pieces counted "1 & 2 & 3 &".
  - M3–4: click on every beat; thumb locks to click. Tap foot. Start the "Wednesday pause" diagnostic already in the plan.
  - M5: sixteenth subdivision spoken ("1 e & a") over a steady thumb; hum melody while tapping pulse (already in M5 ear).
  - M6–7: click on 2 and 4 at half tempo for alternating bass pieces; then "gap click" (2 bars on, 2 bars silent) to test drift.
  - M8–9: add the percussive backbeat (thumb slap or palm/nail hit on 2 and 4) with the click on 2 and 4 so the slap lines up with the click — the core Jung/Oshio groove.
  - M10–12: play along with the original pop recording (beat induction from real music, not a click), and record against a click-free take to check tempo drift.
- Because timing is movement-specific, count/tap exercises away from the guitar should be short; most pulse work should be on the instrument.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found directly comparing "metronome on 2 and 4" vs "on every beat" for learning outcomes; that recommendation is pedagogical, not experimental.
- No study found comparing metronome-dependent practice vs internal-pulse practice for adult beginners.

## Theory for arranging, and step-by-step arranging methods

### Takeaway
An adult can arrange an 8–16-bar pop melody in year one using only: the major-key diatonic chords as numbers (1, 2m, 3m, 4, 5, 6m), an open-string key (C, G, D, A, E), melody placed on strings 1–3 above a root (or root–5th) bass, and a short intro/ending built from the song's hook; published fingerstyle methods (de Grassi, Guitareo, Knowler, Emmanuel) all follow melody-first, then bass, then fill.

### Cited Findings
- Nashville Number System: chords written as scale-degree numbers; in every major key 1, 4, 5 are major, 2, 3, 6 minor, 7 diminished; minor marked "6-" or "6m". "1 4 5" in C is C–F–G; in D it is D–G–A — so a chart transposes instantly. Originated with the Jordanaires. — [Wikipedia: Nashville Number System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System); [Sweetwater InSync: NNS demystified](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/the-nashville-number-system-demystified/)
- Alex de Grassi (arranging "Shenandoah"): (1) identify the melody within the chord progression, (2) highlight the melody as single-note phrases, (3) combine into a complete solo. — [Acoustic Guitar: Tips to Create a Solo Fingerstyle Arrangement](https://acousticguitar.com/tips-to-create-a-solo-fingerstyle-guitar-arrangement/)
- Guitareo/GuitarLessons.com ("Yesterday"): (1) listen and find the chords, (2) build a bass line from roots and fifths alternating in eighth notes, (3) write the melody on higher strings, placed to be playable over the bass, (4) combine and practise, raising tempo gradually; Guitar Pro used to lay out the arrangement. — [GuitarLessons.com: Arranging Solo Fingerstyle Songs](https://www.guitarlessons.com/guitar-lessons/guitar-lessons-for-beginners/arranging-solo-fingerstyle-songs/)
- Cameron Knowler (traditional tunes): keep melody in the upper part of common chord shapes (useful for vocal tunes); use quarter-note scalar/chromatic bass runs to link chords; use syncopation sparingly as anticipation between chord changes; tailor devices to the melody's contour. — [Acoustic Guitar: Techniques for Arranging Traditional Tunes](https://acousticguitar.com/techniques-for-arranging-traditional-tunes-on-guitar/)
- Tommy Emmanuel: stays "true to the melody" but tries to do something personal with it; sings the melody first and learns how singers phrase it. — [Guitar World](https://www.guitarworld.com/features/tommy-emmanuel-tips-for-acoustic-guitar); [Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/m-always-true-melody-tommy-091500549.html)
- Chad Johnson (Hal Leonard) has a beginner Fingerstyle Guitar Method (songs from Beatles, James Taylor, Paul Simon etc.) and *How to Fingerpick Songs on Guitar: Essential Patterns, Techniques & Arranging Concepts* (ISBN 9781495056352). — [Goodreads: Hal Leonard Fingerstyle Guitar Method](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/21394842-fingerstyle-guitar-hal-leonard-guitar-method); [Amazon: How to Fingerpick Songs on Guitar](https://www.amazon.com/How-Fingerpick-Songs-Guitar-Techniques/dp/149505635X)
- Sungha Jung learned composing/arranging from Ulli Bögershausen and arranges covers largely by ear. — [Wikipedia: Sungha Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sungha_Jung)

### Inferences
- Month-8 method (8–16 bars, ~2–4 weeks, uses the full music block):
  1. Pick a simple, singable melody (a chorus hook) and sing it until secure (Emmanuel).
  2. Choose a key: try C, G, D first; pick the one where the highest melody note sits at or below about fret 5–7 on string 1 and the lowest melody note stays on strings 1–3 (so the bass has room below). Use a capo to match the original singer's key later if needed.
  3. Find the melody by ear on strings 1–3 in that key (ear block M6–7 skill).
  4. Write the chord numbers above each bar (1, 4, 5, 6m cover most pop choruses; NNS).
  5. Bass: one root on beat 1 per chord; then root–5th alternating (Guitareo) if the hand allows.
  6. Voicing: the melody note must be the highest note; if a chord shape puts a higher note above it, drop that note (de Grassi "melody within the chord").
  7. Fill only where the melody holds a long note (1–2 inner chord tones).
  8. Intro: play the last 2 bars of the hook, or the hook's chords with bass + one melody note; Outro: repeat the final phrase and end on the 1 chord with the melody on 1 (or 3/5 for colour).
  9. Record it, sing along to check the melody projects.
- Month 9 "colour without losing the tune": add one device at a time — a quarter-note bass run between two chords (Knowler), a hammer-on in the melody, a 2/4 percussive slap, a sus2/sus4 or add9 shape on the 1 or 4 chord only where the melody note isn't changed — and keep an A/B recording "plain vs coloured".
- Month 10 "dream-piece experiment": transcribe the Jung/Oshio intro or main motif (4–8 bars) and label its chords with numbers to see how the pro harmonised it.

## Memorisation, performance anxiety, mistake recovery, and home recording

### Takeaway
Chaffin's research shows reliable memorised performance depends on deliberately planned "performance cues" (structural and expressive landmarks) you can restart from, not just motor chaining; combine this with performance simulation (recording, mild stress, small audiences) and a "never stop the beat" recovery rule for Month 11, and a phone placed about 6–18 inches from the 12th–14th fret for Month 12.

### Cited Findings
- Two memorisation approaches: serial cueing (each phrase triggers the next; fragile under nerves) vs content-addressable access through deliberately created performance cues, allowing recovery at many points. Chaffin et al. (2009): cues are "created during practice sessions, and are rehearsed". Cue types: structural, expressive, interpretive, basic (technical); structural and expressive cues are most effective. Practice by starting and stopping at recovery points. — [Bulletproof Musician: Two Approaches to Memorization](https://bulletproofmusician.com/musicians-seem-memorization-naturals-can-become-one/)
- Novices encode a piece mainly as motor and auditory memory; experts add a conceptual (declarative) map via performance cues. — [Chaffin, Demos & Logan 2016, "Performing from Memory" (PDF)](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2018/01/2016-Chaffin-Demos-Logan-Performing_from_Memory.pdf)
- Chaffin, Ginsborg, Dixon & Demos (2023) studied recovery from memory failure, linking recovery to musical structure and performance cues. — [Musicae Scientiae 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10298649211025491) (abstract not retrievable, 403)
- Secure memorisation was linked to expressive freedom in a longitudinal case study (Chopin Barcarolle). — [Chaffin, Gerling & Demos 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/10298649241241405)
- Recovery advice (Laurel Thomsen): continue without drawing attention — don't grimace, never miss the next beat; handle big lapses honestly/with humour; in the second half of preparation, record yourself to induce nerves, practise with a distracting audience, play in varied rooms, and do aerobic exercise before run-throughs to simulate adrenaline. — [Strings Magazine: Recovering Onstage After a Mistake](https://stringsmagazine.com/recovering-onstage-after-a-mistake-and-how-to-prepare-your-pieces-to-help-avoid-it/)
- Noa Kageyama (Juilliard, PhD psychology) similarly recommends simulating performance arousal (e.g., exercise before a run) and a deliberately distracting audience. — [Musical U: Becoming a Bulletproof Musician](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/becoming-a-bulletproof-musician-with-noa-kageyama/)
- Music performance anxiety prevalence estimates vary widely (roughly 16.5–60%, with some reports up to 96%); coping includes self-talk, cognitive restructuring, breathing and practice strategies; systematic review finds CBT, mindfulness and ACT interventions reduce MPA. — [PMC12062091 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12062091); [Frontiers 2023 symptoms and coping](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1138922/full)
- In young amateur musicians, about three quarters fell into a "positive" profile with low symptoms, high self-efficacy and functional coping. — [PMC10425269](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10425269/) (search summary; age range of sample not verified)
- Recording: aim the mic at the 12th–14th fret, about 6–12 inches away, for a natural balance; point toward the neck for thinner/brighter, toward the bridge for warmer; avoid the soundhole (boomy); beyond ~12 inches you get more room sound. — [Audient: 5 tips for recording acoustic guitar](https://audient.com/tutorial/5-essential-tips-for-recording-acoustic-guitar/); [Music Guy Mixing: how to mic an acoustic](https://www.musicguymixing.com/how-to-mic-an-acoustic-guitar/); [Sweetwater: pro acoustic techniques](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/acoustic-guitar-recording-techniques-pros/). One source suggests 12–18 inches from where neck meets body for a phone. — [Music Guy Mixing: 14 tips](https://www.musicguymixing.com/how-to-record-acoustic-guitar/)

### Inferences
- Month 11 memory routine (5–10 min): mark 3–5 "landing spots" per piece (start of each section = structural; one "feel" cue per section = expressive); practise starting from each spot at random; one daily "no-stop" run where any slip must be followed by the next landing spot on the beat.
- Weekly performance simulation from Month 6: phone recording counts as an audience; in Month 11 add one 60-second jog/stairs before a set run, and one run for a friend/family member.
- Audiation check: play the piece mentally without the guitar once a day (combined-practice evidence above).
- Month 12 recording checklist: quiet room with soft furnishings; phone in airplane mode on a stand, 6–18 in from the 12th–14th fret; do a 10-second test for clipping (no distortion on loud slaps); record three full takes, keep the best, don't edit notes; note date/tempo in the log. Compare with Month 1 recording.
- Adult amateurs mostly have a healthy anxiety profile; normalising nerves and using breathing/self-talk are sufficient; clinical approaches aren't needed unless anxiety is disabling.

## Reading: TAB plus rhythm notation vs standard notation in year one

### Takeaway
No direct evidence was found on this question; for a pop-arranging, fingerstyle goal, TAB with rhythm stems plus chord numbers is sufficient in year one, with standard-notation rhythm reading (not pitch reading) as the useful add-on.

### Cited Findings
- Jung's workflow is ear- and video-based rather than notation-based. — [Wikipedia: Sungha Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sungha_Jung)
- Arranging tutorials use TAB-and-notation software (Guitar Pro) to lay out arrangements. — [GuitarLessons.com](https://www.guitarlessons.com/guitar-lessons/guitar-lessons-for-beginners/arranging-solo-fingerstyle-songs/)
- NNS chord charts provide a notation for harmony/structure without staff notation. — [Wikipedia: NNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System)

### Inferences
- Learn to read rhythm values (whole to sixteenth, dots, ties, rests) as they appear on TAB stems in M1–3; write arrangements (M8) as TAB + rhythm + chord numbers in free software (e.g., MuseScore, or Guitar Pro).
- Standard pitch reading is optional in year one; revisit in year two if interested in classical repertoire.

### Gaps
- No research found comparing TAB-only vs staff-notation learning outcomes for adult guitarists.
